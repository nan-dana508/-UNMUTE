Your Emotional Wellbeing Matters

A cancer diagnosis can bring many emotions, and there is no single right way to experience them. Whether you're feeling anxious, overwhelmed, uncertain or simply looking for someone who understands, this space is here to help.

Your feelings deserve care, understanding and support too.

Understanding Emotional Wellbeing

Emotional wellbeing is about understanding your feelings, finding ways to cope with challenges and having support when you need it. It does not mean feeling happy all the time or being unaffected by difficult experiences.

Cancer can affect more than physical health. A diagnosis and its treatment may bring feelings such as shock, sadness, anger, fear, uncertainty, loneliness or even relief at finally having answers.

Why do emotions change?

Your feelings may change throughout diagnosis, treatment, recovery and life after treatment. Some days may feel manageable, while others may be more difficult. Everyone responds differently.

What might help?

  • Give yourself permission to acknowledge what you feel.
  • Talk with someone you trust when you feel ready.
  • Ask your healthcare team questions when something worries you.
  • Consider counselling or a support group if you would like additional support.
  • Allow yourself time to adjust without comparing your experience with someone else's.

There is no correct way to feel about cancer. You do not have to remain positive every moment, and you do not have to manage everything alone.

Coping With a Cancer Diagnosis

Hearing that you have cancer can be overwhelming. You may have many questions about treatment, your health, your family, education, work and what happens next.

Some people feel shocked or numb. Others feel frightened, angry, confused or uncertain. You may experience several emotions at once, and your feelings may change over time.

Taking things one step at a time

  • Give yourself time to process the information.
  • Write down questions before appointments so you don't have to remember everything.
  • Ask your doctor to explain unfamiliar terms or treatment options.
  • Consider taking a trusted person to appointments, if you would find that helpful.
  • Decide whom you want to tell about your diagnosis and when.
  • Ask for practical or emotional support instead of feeling you must manage everything yourself.

When everything feels overwhelming

You do not need to understand every part of your treatment immediately. Ask your healthcare team to explain the next step and focus on the information you need right now.

You do not need to be brave every moment. Asking questions, expressing fear and accepting help are all valid ways of coping.

Managing Anxiety, Fear and Uncertainty

Worry and uncertainty can be part of living with cancer. You may feel anxious about treatment, medical appointments, symptoms, recovery or what the future might look like.

How might anxiety feel?

Anxiety can affect people differently. Some experiences include persistent worrying, racing thoughts, restlessness, difficulty concentrating, sleep changes or physical tension. These experiences can also have physical or treatment-related causes, so discuss new or concerning symptoms with your healthcare team.

Small ways to cope

  • Focus on the next manageable step rather than trying to solve everything at once.
  • Write down worries and questions you would like to discuss with your care team.
  • Take breaks from information or online searches if they increase your distress.
  • Talk to someone you trust instead of keeping every worry to yourself.
  • Try a calming activity, such as gentle breathing or listening to music.
  • Maintain a flexible routine that allows time for rest and activities you enjoy.

When to ask for support

If anxiety feels difficult to manage, continues to affect your sleep or everyday life, or interferes with treatment, speak with your healthcare team. A mental-health professional can help you explore suitable coping strategies.

You do not have to figure out the entire future today. It is okay to focus on what you need in this moment.

Scan Anxiety and Fear of Recurrence

Medical scans and follow-up appointments can bring considerable stress. The term scanxiety is often used to describe the anxiety people experience before, during or after medical scans.

Why can scans feel stressful?

  • Worry about what the results might show.
  • Uncertainty about whether treatment is working.
  • Waiting for reports or follow-up appointments.
  • Remembering difficult experiences from earlier tests or treatment.
  • Worrying about cancer returning after treatment.

Preparing for a scan or appointment

  • Ask your healthcare team what the scan involves and when results may be available.
  • Write down questions you want to ask about the results.
  • If possible, arrange for someone you trust to accompany you.
  • Plan something comforting or enjoyable for after the appointment.
  • Try not to interpret medical reports without discussing them with your doctor.

Fear of recurrence

Worry about cancer returning can continue after treatment and may become stronger around follow-up appointments. Having this fear does not, by itself, mean that cancer has returned.

Discuss new, persistent or concerning symptoms with your healthcare team rather than trying to determine their cause on your own.

Waiting for answers can be difficult. You deserve support during the waiting period too.

Body Image and Self-Confidence

Cancer and its treatment can bring physical changes that affect how you see yourself. These changes may influence confidence, relationships and your sense of identity.

Changes you may experience

  • Hair loss or changes in hair growth.
  • Changes in weight or body shape.
  • Surgical scars or changes following surgery.
  • Changes in skin, nails or physical appearance.
  • Changes involving fertility or reproductive health.
  • Fatigue or other physical changes that affect everyday activities.

Being kinder to yourself

  • Allow yourself to have mixed feelings about physical changes.
  • Explore clothing, scarves, wigs or other options if they make you feel comfortable.
  • Talk with someone you trust about changes that are difficult for you.
  • Ask your care team about physical changes, fertility and reproductive-health concerns.
  • Seek counselling if concerns about appearance are affecting your everyday life or relationships.

You do not have to love every change or feel confident all the time. Your experience and feelings deserve respect.

Your appearance does not determine your worth. You deserve care and respect at every stage of your journey.

Relationships, Friendships and Communication

A cancer diagnosis can affect relationships in different ways. Some people may feel closer to their loved ones, while others may experience misunderstandings, distance or difficulty communicating.

Friends and family may want to help but might not know what to say or what you need. You may also find it difficult to explain what you are going through.

Communicating your needs

  • Share information about your diagnosis at your own pace.
  • Tell people what kind of support would actually help you.
  • It is okay to say when you do not want to talk about cancer.
  • Set boundaries around advice, questions and visitors.
  • Stay connected in ways that feel manageable to you.
  • Consider asking someone you trust to help communicate updates to others.

Romantic relationships and intimacy

Cancer and its treatment may affect intimacy, sexuality, fertility and how partners relate to one another. These concerns are valid and can be discussed with your partner and healthcare team when you feel ready.

You are allowed to ask for understanding, space and respect. You do not owe everyone every detail of your experience.

Counselling and Professional Support

Sometimes talking with friends or family may not feel like enough. Professional emotional support can help you work through difficult feelings, understand your concerns and explore ways to cope.

Who can provide support?

  • Psycho-oncologists: Professionals who focus on the psychological and social aspects of cancer.
  • Psychologists: Professionals who can provide psychological assessment and therapy.
  • Psychiatrists: Medical doctors who specialise in mental health and can assess whether medication or other treatment may help.
  • Counsellors: Trained professionals who offer support with emotional and personal challenges. Their qualifications and scope of practice can vary.
  • Social workers: Professionals who may help with practical, family, financial and support-service needs.

What can you discuss?

  • Fear, anxiety and uncertainty about cancer.
  • Body image and changes in self-confidence.
  • Relationships, family concerns and communication.
  • Stress related to treatment, education or work.
  • Adjusting to life after treatment.

How can you find support?

  • Ask your oncologist whether the hospital offers psycho-oncology or counselling services.
  • Ask for a referral to a qualified mental-health professional.
  • Explore reputable hospital or community support programmes.
  • Ask about available subsidised or low-cost services.

Counselling is not only for people experiencing a crisis. You can seek support whenever you feel it might help. Ask the service about confidentiality and its limits before sharing personal information.

Asking for professional help is not a sign of weakness. Your emotional wellbeing deserves attention too.

Emotional Wellbeing for Young Patients

Being diagnosed with cancer at a young age can bring challenges that affect many parts of life. Alongside treatment, you may be thinking about education, career plans, friendships, independence, relationships and the future.

Challenges young people may face

  • Taking a break from school, college or work.
  • Feeling disconnected from friends or people of the same age.
  • Worrying about future plans and independence.
  • Changes in appearance, relationships or self-confidence.
  • Questions about fertility and reproductive health.
  • Feeling that life is moving differently from what you expected.

Finding support that understands your needs

  • Ask your educational institution about academic flexibility or leave arrangements.
  • Stay connected with friends in ways that suit your energy and comfort.
  • Explore age-appropriate cancer support groups and peer communities.
  • Discuss fertility and reproductive-health questions with your care team.
  • Consider counselling if you feel isolated or overwhelmed.
  • Give yourself permission to pursue personal interests and goals at your own pace.

Cancer is one part of your life. It does not define your identity, erase your ambitions or make your future less worthy of dreaming about.

Everyday Coping and Self-Care

Self-care does not have to involve complicated routines or large changes. It can simply mean recognising what you need and finding manageable ways to support your physical and emotional wellbeing.

Small things that may help

  • Create a flexible daily routine that allows time for rest.
  • Stay connected with people who make you feel comfortable.
  • Make space for activities you enjoy, such as music, reading, art or journaling.
  • Try relaxation activities or gentle breathing when they feel helpful.
  • Respect your energy levels and take breaks when needed.
  • Spend time outdoors if you feel well enough and your care team says it is suitable.
  • Ask for help with everyday tasks when you need it.

On difficult days

Some days may not go according to plan. You may have less energy, feel upset or simply want to rest. You do not need to turn every day into a productive one.

During treatment, discuss new exercise routines, major dietary changes or complementary practices with your healthcare team before starting them.

Resting, doing less and asking someone for help can be forms of self-care too.

When to Seek Professional Help

Difficult emotions can be part of a cancer experience. However, when distress becomes persistent, overwhelming or begins affecting everyday life, additional support may be helpful.

When might you need extra support?

Consider speaking with a healthcare professional if you experience:

  • Persistent sadness, hopelessness or emotional numbness.
  • Anxiety or worry that feels difficult to control.
  • Ongoing sleep difficulties.
  • Loss of interest in activities you previously enjoyed.
  • Difficulty concentrating or managing everyday responsibilities.
  • Withdrawing from friends, family or other sources of support.
  • Feelings of guilt, worthlessness or being a burden.
  • Emotional distress that affects relationships or makes treatment harder to manage.

These experiences do not automatically mean that you have a mental-health condition. A qualified professional can help understand what is happening and discuss appropriate support.

Where can you begin?

  • Tell your oncologist or another member of your treatment team how you have been feeling.
  • Ask about a psycho-oncologist, psychologist or psychiatrist.
  • Explore counselling services through your hospital or a reputable organisation.
