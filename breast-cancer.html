01 — THE BASICS

What is Breast Cancer?

Breast cancer is a disease in which abnormal cells in the breast grow uncontrollably and form a tumour. It most commonly begins in the milk ducts or milk-producing lobules.

Some breast cancers remain within their original location, while others invade surrounding breast tissue and may spread to nearby lymph nodes or distant parts of the body.

Breast cancer is not one single disease. Different types behave differently, and understanding the specific type helps doctors plan treatment.

02 — TYPES AND SUBTYPES

Understanding the Different Types

Breast cancers are classified in several ways, including where they begin, whether they have invaded surrounding tissue, and their biological characteristics.

Based on where they begin

  • Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS): Abnormal cells are found inside the milk ducts but have not invaded nearby breast tissue. It is also called stage 0 breast cancer.
  • Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC): Cancer begins in a milk duct and spreads into the surrounding breast tissue. It is the most common type of invasive breast cancer.
  • Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC): Cancer begins in the milk-producing lobules and spreads into surrounding breast tissue.

Based on biological characteristics

Doctors also examine certain proteins and hormone receptors in cancer cells. These results help identify the cancer subtype.

  • Hormone receptor-positive (HR+): Cancer cells have estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), or both. Hormone or endocrine therapy may be useful.
  • HER2-positive: Cancer cells have higher-than-normal levels of the HER2 protein. HER2-targeted treatments may be an option.
  • Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC): Cancer cells do not have estrogen receptors, progesterone receptors, or high levels of HER2. Treatment approaches differ from hormone receptor-positive and HER2-positive cancers.

These categories can overlap. For example, a breast cancer can be both hormone receptor-positive and HER2-positive.

03 — RECOGNISING CHANGES

Signs and Symptoms

Breast cancer can cause changes in the breast or surrounding areas. Some people experience no noticeable symptoms, particularly in the early stages.

Possible signs and symptoms include:

  • A new lump or thickening in the breast or underarm.
  • A change in the size, shape or appearance of a breast.
  • Dimpling, puckering or changes in the breast skin.
  • Redness, swelling or unusual changes in the skin.
  • A newly inverted nipple or other changes in nipple appearance.
  • Unusual nipple discharge, particularly bloody discharge.
  • Persistent changes around the nipple or areola.
Important to remember

Most breast lumps are not cancer. However, a new or unusual breast change should be assessed by a qualified healthcare professional, even if it is painless.

Do not wait for a lump to become painful before seeking medical advice.

04 — UNDERSTANDING RISK

Risk Factors

A risk factor is something that may increase a person's likelihood of developing a disease. Having one or more risk factors does not mean someone will definitely develop breast cancer.

Factors associated with increased risk

  • Age: The risk generally increases as a person gets older.
  • Family history: Having close relatives with breast or certain other cancers may increase risk.
  • Inherited gene changes: Changes in genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2 and PALB2 can significantly increase risk.
  • Personal medical history: Certain previous breast conditions or a history of breast cancer may increase risk.
  • Radiation exposure: Previous radiation treatment involving the chest, particularly at a young age, may increase risk.
  • Hormonal and reproductive factors: Certain reproductive patterns and use of menopausal hormone therapy may influence risk.
  • Lifestyle and other factors: Alcohol consumption, tobacco use and obesity are among the factors associated with increased risk.
Having no family history does not mean having no risk.

Many people diagnosed with breast cancer have no known family history of the disease. Breast cancer can also occur in younger people.

05 — EARLY DETECTION

Breast Cancer Screening

Breast cancer screening involves checking for signs of breast cancer in people who do not have noticeable symptoms. Its purpose is to identify possible cancers before they cause symptoms, when earlier treatment may be possible.

Screening recommendations depend on factors such as age, personal and family medical history, individual risk, and the healthcare guidelines followed in a particular country.

Common screening methods

  • Mammography: A mammogram is an X-ray image of the breast. It can identify certain abnormalities, including small masses and calcium deposits, sometimes before they can be felt.
  • Clinical Breast Examination (CBE): A trained healthcare professional examines the breasts and nearby areas for unusual lumps, thickening or other changes.
  • Additional imaging for selected individuals: Ultrasound or breast MRI may be recommended in particular situations, such as when further assessment is needed or when someone has a significantly increased risk.

Who should consider screening?

There is no single screening schedule that applies to everyone. Recommendations differ across countries and healthcare organisations.

For example, the United States Preventive Services Task Force recommends a mammogram every two years for women aged 40 to 74 who fall within the population covered by its guidance.

In India, government population-based screening materials have included Clinical Breast Examination as a method for women aged 30 to 65. Local availability and implementation should be confirmed with a healthcare provider or public health facility.

People with a strong family history, inherited genetic changes such as BRCA1 or BRCA2, previous high-dose radiation exposure to the chest, or certain previous breast conditions may need an individualised screening plan.

Screening vs. diagnostic testing

These terms are related, but they do not mean the same thing.

  • Screening: Testing someone who does not have noticeable breast symptoms to look for possible signs of cancer.
  • Diagnostic testing: Investigations performed when someone has a breast change, an abnormal screening result, or another finding that needs further evaluation.
Important: Do not wait for a screening appointment.

If you notice a new breast lump, unusual nipple discharge, skin changes, swelling or another persistent breast change, seek medical evaluation. You may need diagnostic tests even if you are younger than the usual screening age.

What are the limitations of screening?

Screening can help detect breast cancer, but no screening test is perfect. It is important to understand its possible limitations.

  • False-positive results: A screening test may show an abnormality that turns out not to be cancer. Further imaging or a biopsy may be needed.
  • False-negative results: A screening test may not detect a cancer that is actually present.
  • Overdiagnosis: Screening may identify some cancers that would not have caused symptoms or affected a person's health during their lifetime.
Breast awareness matters too.

Becoming familiar with what is normal for your breasts can help you notice unusual changes. Breast awareness is useful, but it does not replace recommended screening or medical evaluation.

06 — MEDICAL EVALUATION

How Is Breast Cancer Diagnosed?

Diagnosing breast cancer involves several steps. Doctors combine medical history, physical examination, imaging tests and, when necessary, a biopsy to understand an abnormality and determine whether it is cancer.

Not everyone needs every test. The investigations recommended depend on the person's symptoms, examination findings and previous test results.

1. Clinical Breast Examination

A healthcare professional examines the breasts, chest and underarm areas for lumps, thickening, skin changes, nipple changes or enlarged lymph nodes. They may also ask about symptoms, personal medical history and family history.

2. Breast Imaging

Imaging tests help doctors examine breast tissue and identify areas that may need further investigation.

  • Diagnostic mammography: Uses X-ray images to investigate a breast abnormality or an unusual screening result. Additional views may be taken to examine a particular area more closely.
  • Breast ultrasound: Uses sound waves to produce images of breast tissue. It can help distinguish fluid-filled cysts from solid masses and guide certain biopsy procedures.
  • Breast MRI: Uses magnetic fields and radio waves to create detailed breast images. It may be recommended in selected situations, including certain high-risk assessments or further evaluation of known breast cancer.

3. Breast Biopsy

Imaging can identify suspicious areas, but it cannot always determine whether an abnormality is cancer. A biopsy involves collecting a small sample of tissue so that it can be examined under a microscope.

Common biopsy methods include:

  • Core needle biopsy: A hollow needle is used to collect small samples of breast tissue. It is commonly performed with imaging guidance.
  • Fine needle aspiration (FNA): A thin needle removes cells or fluid from an area of concern. It may help investigate certain lumps or lymph nodes, but it does not provide the same tissue architecture as a core biopsy.
  • Surgical biopsy: A procedure in which part or all of an abnormal area is removed for examination. It may be considered when other tests do not provide enough information.
Why is a biopsy important?

A biopsy allows a pathologist to examine cells and tissue directly. It is usually needed to confirm whether a suspicious breast abnormality is cancer and to identify important features of the disease.

4. Pathology and Histopathology

After a biopsy, the tissue sample is examined by a pathologist. The resulting pathology report provides information about the cells and tissue.

A report may describe:

  • Whether cancer is present: The examination helps determine whether the sampled tissue contains cancer cells.
  • Cancer type: It may identify the type of breast cancer, such as invasive ductal carcinoma or invasive lobular carcinoma.
  • Tumour grade: Describes how abnormal the cancer cells look under a microscope and gives an indication of how they may behave.
  • Invasion: Helps establish whether cancer cells have invaded surrounding breast tissue.

5. Understanding ER, PR and HER2 Testing

If breast cancer is confirmed, additional tests are often performed to identify biological characteristics that can influence treatment.

  • ER (Estrogen Receptor): Determines whether cancer cells have receptors for the hormone estrogen.
  • PR (Progesterone Receptor): Determines whether cancer cells have receptors for the hormone progesterone.
  • HER2 (Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2): Evaluates whether cancer cells have increased levels of the HER2 protein or gene amplification.

These results help classify breast cancer into biologically meaningful groups and can help doctors determine whether hormone therapy or HER2-targeted treatment may be appropriate.

6. Further Tests When Needed

If breast cancer is confirmed, additional investigations may be recommended to assess the extent of disease or plan treatment.

Depending on the situation, these may include blood tests, lymph node evaluation or imaging studies such as CT, PET-CT or bone scans. Not everyone needs all of these investigations.

A diagnosis is based on the complete picture.

No single imaging result or symptom should be interpreted in isolation. Doctors consider clinical findings, imaging and pathology together when making a diagnosis and planning further care.

07 — UNDERSTANDING PROGRESSION

Stages of Breast Cancer

Breast cancer staging describes how extensive the cancer is in the body. It helps doctors understand the extent of the disease, discuss treatment options and estimate prognosis.

Breast cancer is commonly classified into stages 0 to IV. The stage is determined using information about the primary tumour, nearby lymph nodes and whether the cancer has spread to distant parts of the body.

Understanding the TNM system

Doctors use a classification system called TNM to describe the extent of breast cancer. Each letter represents a different feature:

  • T — Tumour: Describes the size of the primary tumour and whether it has grown into nearby structures.
  • N — Nodes: Describes whether cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes and, if so, how much involvement is present.
  • M — Metastasis: Indicates whether cancer has spread to distant parts of the body, such as the bones, lungs, liver or brain.

The stages of breast cancer

The following descriptions provide a general introduction. The exact stage depends on detailed clinical findings and staging criteria.

  • Stage 0 — Non-invasive disease: Abnormal cells are present but have not invaded surrounding breast tissue. Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is an example.
  • Stage I — Early invasive cancer: Cancer is relatively small and may have spread to a limited number of nearby lymph nodes, depending on the specific stage classification.
  • Stage II — Larger or more locally involved cancer: The tumour may be larger, or cancer may have spread to nearby lymph nodes.
  • Stage III — Locally advanced cancer: Cancer may involve more nearby lymph nodes or extend into surrounding skin or the chest wall. Some forms, including inflammatory breast cancer, are generally classified as stage III when there is no distant spread.
  • Stage IV — Metastatic breast cancer: Cancer has spread beyond the breast and nearby lymph nodes to distant parts of the body, such as the bones, lungs, liver or brain.
What does metastatic mean?

Metastatic breast cancer means that cancer cells have spread from the original breast tumour to another part of the body. It is also called stage IV breast cancer.

Even when breast cancer spreads to another organ, it is still considered breast cancer because of where it originally began.

Clinical staging and pathological staging

Staging can be assessed at different points during diagnosis and treatment.

  • Clinical stage: Determined using information from physical examinations, imaging and biopsies before treatment begins.
  • Pathological stage: Determined using findings from tissue examined after surgery. It can provide additional information about the tumour and lymph nodes.

When treatment such as chemotherapy is given before surgery, doctors may also assess how much cancer remains after treatment. This information can help guide further care.

Stage and grade are not the same

These terms are sometimes confused, but they describe different characteristics of cancer.

  • Stage describes how far the cancer has spread in the body.
  • Grade describes how abnormal the cancer cells look under a microscope and can provide information about how they may grow.

Doctors may also consider hormone receptor status, HER2 status and other tumour features when determining the overall stage group and planning treatment.

A stage does not tell the whole story.

A higher stage does not mean that treatment is pointless, and a lower stage does not guarantee that cancer will never return. Treatment decisions depend on several factors, including cancer subtype, tumour characteristics, overall health and individual circumstances.

Every person's diagnosis and treatment plan is different. A healthcare team can explain what a particular stage means in an individual situation.

08 — TREATMENT OPTIONS

Understanding Breast Cancer Treatment

Breast cancer treatment aims to remove or destroy cancer cells, control the disease, reduce the risk of recurrence or manage symptoms. The approach depends on the individual person's diagnosis and circumstances.

There is no single treatment plan for everyone. Doctors consider several factors before recommending treatment, including:

  • The stage and extent of the cancer.
  • The type and grade of the tumour.
  • Hormone receptor (ER and PR) status.
  • HER2 status and other relevant tumour markers.
  • The person's age, general health and menopausal status.
  • Individual preferences and treatment goals.

1. Surgery

Surgery is an important treatment for many people with breast cancer. It involves removing the cancer from the breast and, when necessary, evaluating nearby lymph nodes.

  • Breast-conserving surgery (Lumpectomy): The surgeon removes the cancer along with a surrounding margin of healthy tissue while preserving most of the breast.
  • Mastectomy: The surgeon removes the entire breast. Different types of mastectomy are available, and breast reconstruction may be an option for some people.
  • Sentinel lymph node biopsy: A procedure used to examine the first lymph node or nodes to which cancer is most likely to spread from the breast.
  • Axillary lymph node surgery: In selected situations, additional lymph nodes in the underarm area may need to be removed and examined.

The choice between breast-conserving surgery and mastectomy depends on factors such as tumour size, location, disease extent, previous treatment and individual circumstances. Neither option is automatically appropriate for everyone.

2. Radiation Therapy

Radiation therapy uses high-energy radiation to destroy cancer cells or damage their ability to grow. It is directed at a particular area of the body.

It is commonly recommended after breast-conserving surgery to reduce the risk of cancer returning in the breast. It may also be recommended after mastectomy in certain situations, depending on tumour and lymph node findings.

Radiation therapy may cause side effects such as skin irritation, redness, tiredness and changes in the treated area. The effects vary between individuals and depend on the treatment plan.

3. Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy uses medicines to destroy cancer cells or stop them from growing and dividing. These medicines can travel through the bloodstream and reach different parts of the body.

Chemotherapy may be given at different points:

  • Neoadjuvant chemotherapy: Given before surgery to shrink a tumour, make surgery easier in some situations, or assess how the cancer responds to treatment.
  • Adjuvant chemotherapy: Given after surgery to destroy any remaining cancer cells that may not be visible and reduce the risk of recurrence.
  • Chemotherapy for advanced disease: May be used to control cancer that has spread to other parts of the body, depending on the cancer subtype and previous treatments.

Not everyone with breast cancer needs chemotherapy. Its potential benefits and risks are assessed according to the characteristics of the cancer and the person's overall situation.

What about chemotherapy side effects?

Possible side effects include tiredness, nausea, hair loss, changes in blood cell counts and an increased risk of infections. The effects depend on the medicines used and vary from person to person. Supportive medicines and other measures can help manage many side effects.

4. Hormone or Endocrine Therapy

Some breast cancers use hormones such as estrogen or progesterone to support their growth. These are called hormone receptor-positive cancers.

Endocrine therapy works by blocking the effects of these hormones or reducing the amount of hormone available to cancer cells.

Examples include:

  • Tamoxifen: Blocks estrogen receptors in breast tissue and may be used in certain hormone receptor-positive breast cancers.
  • Aromatase inhibitors: Medicines such as anastrozole, letrozole and exemestane reduce estrogen production in the body. They are commonly used in postmenopausal patients.
  • Ovarian suppression: In some premenopausal patients, treatment may be used to reduce ovarian hormone production, sometimes alongside other endocrine medicines.

Endocrine therapy is generally considered for hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. It is not a treatment for every breast cancer subtype.

5. Targeted Therapy

Targeted therapies are medicines designed to act on particular proteins, genes or other features that help cancer cells grow and survive. They are used when the relevant target is present and the treatment is appropriate.

  • HER2-targeted therapy: Medicines such as trastuzumab target the HER2 protein. They are used in certain HER2-positive breast cancers, often as part of a combination treatment plan.
  • CDK4/6 inhibitors: Medicines such as palbociclib, ribociclib and abemaciclib may be used with endocrine therapy in certain hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancers.
  • PARP inhibitors: Medicines such as olaparib may be considered for selected patients with particular inherited BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene changes.

The availability and suitability of targeted treatments depend on the cancer's biological characteristics, disease setting and previous treatments.

6. Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy helps the body's immune system recognise and fight cancer cells. It works differently from chemotherapy and other medicines that directly target cancer cells.

Certain immunotherapy medicines may be used in selected cases of triple-negative breast cancer. Eligibility depends on factors such as the disease setting, tumour characteristics and the specific treatment protocol.

Immunotherapy is not suitable for every person with breast cancer. It can also cause immune-related side effects, which need medical monitoring.

7. How Are Different Treatments Combined?

Breast cancer treatment often involves more than one approach. The order and combination of treatments depend on the cancer's stage, subtype and response to earlier treatment.

For example, a treatment plan may involve:

  • Surgery followed by radiation therapy and/or medicines, when appropriate.
  • Chemotherapy or targeted treatment before surgery, followed by surgery and additional treatment when indicated.
  • Endocrine therapy for hormone receptor-positive cancer.
  • HER2-targeted medicines for HER2-positive cancer, as part of an appropriate treatment plan.

These are examples rather than fixed treatment sequences. A person's treatment plan may look very different from another person's, even when they have the same cancer stage.

8. Treatment Goals and Supportive Care

Treatment goals depend on the stage and circumstances of the disease. They may include removing the cancer, reducing the risk of recurrence, controlling cancer growth or relieving symptoms.

For metastatic breast cancer, treatment often focuses on controlling the disease, managing symptoms and maintaining quality of life. Treatment options may change over time depending on how the cancer responds.

Supportive care is also an important part of cancer treatment. It may include help with pain, nutrition, physical rehabilitation, emotional well-being and treatment-related side effects.

Palliative care can help improve quality of life and manage symptoms. It can be provided alongside treatments intended to control or eliminate cancer; it is not limited to end-of-life care.

Every treatment journey is different.

The information in this section is intended to explain general treatment approaches, not to recommend a particular medicine or procedure. Treatment decisions should be made with a qualified oncology team after reviewing the complete diagnosis.

Patients can ask their healthcare team about the purpose of each treatment, expected benefits, possible side effects, alternatives and available support.

09 — LOOKING AHEAD

Research & Clinical Trials

Breast cancer research continues to explore better ways to detect, understand and treat the disease. Researchers are studying new medicines, treatment combinations, genetic changes, immunotherapy and ways to reduce the side effects of treatment.

What is breast cancer research?

Research helps scientists and healthcare professionals understand how breast cancer develops, why different cancers respond differently to treatment, and how existing treatments can be improved. Research may involve laboratory studies, observational studies, or clinical trials involving people.

What are clinical trials?

Clinical trials are research studies involving people. They help researchers evaluate new treatments, combinations of existing treatments, diagnostic methods and supportive care approaches.

A clinical trial may investigate a treatment that is not yet approved, or study whether an existing treatment works better in a different situation. Participation does not guarantee personal benefit.

Understanding clinical trial phases

Phase 1

Studies an experimental treatment in a small group to understand its safety, possible side effects and suitable dosage.

Phase 2

Studies whether the treatment shows signs of effectiveness against a particular cancer while continuing to evaluate safety.

Phase 3

Compares the treatment with existing standard treatments in larger groups to assess its effectiveness and safety.

Phase 4

Studies a treatment after approval to learn more about its longer-term effects and safety in wider use.

Areas of ongoing breast cancer research

  • Targeted therapies: Developing treatments that act on particular proteins, genes or pathways involved in cancer.
  • Immunotherapy: Studying ways to help the immune system recognise and fight cancer cells.
  • Personalised treatment: Exploring how tumour characteristics and molecular testing can help guide treatment.
  • Early detection: Improving screening and diagnostic approaches to identify breast cancer earlier.
  • Reducing treatment burden: Studying ways to maintain treatment effectiveness while reducing unnecessary treatment and long-term side effects.

Finding clinical trials

Clinical trial databases allow patients, caregivers and healthcare professionals to explore studies taking place in different locations. Trial availability and eligibility can change, so information should always be checked with the study team.

Before considering a clinical trial

Not every trial is suitable for every patient. Eligibility may depend on cancer type, stage, biomarkers, previous treatments, age and general health.

Questions worth discussing with the treating healthcare team include:

  • What is the purpose of this study?
  • What treatment or intervention is being investigated?
  • What are the known and possible risks?
  • Is the study comparing the treatment with standard care?
  • What additional visits, tests or costs may be involved?
  • Can I leave the study if I change my mind?

Participation should be voluntary and based on informed consent. Patients can ask questions and discuss their options with their healthcare team before making a decision.

Research is not the same as established treatment. A promising laboratory result, early-stage study or individual patient experience does not establish that a treatment is safe or effective. Clinical evidence develops over time, and new findings should be interpreted in context.

This section is for educational purposes. Clinical trial participation and treatment decisions should be discussed with qualified healthcare professionals.

10 — YOU ARE NOT ALONE

Support & Resources

A breast cancer diagnosis can affect many parts of life, not just physical health. Emotional wellbeing, relationships, finances, work and everyday responsibilities may all be affected. Support can come from healthcare professionals, family, friends, patient communities and reliable organisations.

Emotional and mental health support

Fear, uncertainty, sadness, anger and anxiety can be part of the experience of cancer. These feelings deserve care and attention. Speaking with a counsellor, psychologist, social worker or psycho-oncology professional may help patients and caregivers navigate difficult moments.

Support from other patients and survivors

Connecting with people who have experienced breast cancer may help individuals feel less isolated. Peer support can offer understanding, shared experiences and a sense of community.

However, every person's cancer and treatment experience is different. Another person's experience should not be treated as a prediction of what will happen to someone else.

Practical support during treatment

  • Nutrition: Ask a qualified dietitian or healthcare professional about nutritional needs during treatment.
  • Physical activity: Discuss safe and suitable activity with the treating team, especially during recovery or treatment.
  • Financial assistance: Ask hospitals and relevant government programmes about financial support, insurance and treatment assistance.
  • Caregiver support: Family members and caregivers may also need emotional support, rest and practical assistance.
  • Returning to everyday life: Work, education, relationships and daily routines may need adjustments during treatment and recovery.

For family members and caregivers

Caregivers can play an important role by listening without judgement, helping with appointments and everyday tasks, respecting the patient's choices and allowing them space to express their feelings.

Support does not always mean having the right words. Sometimes, being present and listening is enough.

A reminder: No one should have to navigate cancer alone. Asking for help is not a sign of weakness. Patients deserve respectful care, clear information, dignity and support throughout their journey.
