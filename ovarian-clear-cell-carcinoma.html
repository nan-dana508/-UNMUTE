BEYOND THE COMMON

Ovarian Clear Cell Carcinoma.

Understanding a less commonly discussed type of ovarian cancer begins with understanding what makes it different.

Explore its characteristics, signs and symptoms, diagnosis, treatment approaches and the research shaping future possibilities.

01 / UNDERSTANDING THE DIAGNOSIS

What is ovarian clear cell carcinoma?

Ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC) is a distinct histological subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer. It develops from cells in the epithelial tissue associated with the ovary.

It is relatively uncommon compared with the more frequently diagnosed high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma. OCCC has its own characteristic microscopic appearance and molecular features.

Many cases are diagnosed at an early stage. However, advanced-stage disease can be more challenging to treat, which is one reason why continued research into this subtype matters.

Important to know

OCCC is not the same as high-grade serous ovarian cancer or low-grade serous ovarian cancer. These are different histological subtypes, and their characteristics and treatment considerations can differ.

02 / WHAT MAKES IT DIFFERENT?

One organ. Different cancer types.

Ovarian cancers are not all biologically identical. Their appearance under a microscope, molecular characteristics and patterns of behaviour can vary.

Distinct histological features

OCCC is identified by its characteristic appearance under a microscope. Tumour cells may have clear or hobnail-like features. Pathologists use these patterns alongside additional tests to establish the diagnosis.

Different molecular characteristics

OCCC can involve molecular changes that differ from those commonly seen in other ovarian cancer subtypes. Alterations involving genes such as ARID1A and pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR have been studied in this cancer.

Its relationship with endometriosis

OCCC has an established association with endometriosis. However, having endometriosis does not mean someone will develop ovarian cancer, and OCCC can occur without a known history of endometriosis.

03 / RECOGNISING THE SIGNS

Possible signs and symptoms

Symptoms of OCCC can overlap with those of other ovarian conditions. Some people may experience symptoms for some time before receiving a diagnosis.

  • Persistent abdominal bloating or swelling
  • Pelvic or abdominal pain
  • Feeling full quickly when eating
  • Difficulty eating or reduced appetite
  • Changes in urinary habits, including urgency or frequency
  • Changes in bowel habits
  • Unexplained changes in abdominal size
When should you seek medical advice?

Persistent, new or unusual symptoms should be discussed with a healthcare professional, particularly when they occur frequently or represent a change from what is normal for you. These symptoms can have many causes and do not automatically indicate cancer.

04 / DIAGNOSIS AND TESTING

How is OCCC diagnosed?

Diagnosis involves combining clinical assessment, imaging, laboratory investigations and examination of tissue by a pathologist.

Imaging investigations

Depending on the clinical situation, investigations may include pelvic ultrasound, CT scans or MRI. These can help identify an ovarian mass, assess its characteristics and evaluate whether there may be disease elsewhere in the abdomen or pelvis.

Blood tests and tumour markers

CA-125 may be measured as part of an ovarian cancer evaluation and can sometimes help monitor disease during treatment. However, it is not specific to ovarian cancer and cannot establish an OCCC diagnosis by itself.

Pathology and tissue examination

Examination of tumour tissue is essential to establish the cancer subtype. A pathologist assesses the cells under a microscope and may use immunohistochemistry and other tests to distinguish OCCC from other ovarian tumours.

Staging

Once cancer is diagnosed, staging helps describe how far it has spread. Ovarian cancer is commonly staged using the FIGO staging system, which ranges from stage I to stage IV.

The stage, tumour characteristics and findings from surgery and pathology help guide treatment decisions.

05 / TREATMENT APPROACHES

Understanding treatment options

Treatment for OCCC depends on several factors, including the stage of disease, the possibility of safely removing the tumour, the person's overall health, fertility considerations and previous treatment.

Surgery

Surgery is an important part of treatment for many people with OCCC. The surgical approach may involve removing the tumour and assessing the surrounding tissues to determine the extent of disease.

The extent of surgery depends on the individual diagnosis and treatment goals. In selected circumstances, fertility-preserving approaches may be discussed with a specialist.

Chemotherapy

Platinum-based chemotherapy is commonly used in the treatment of OCCC. A combination of carboplatin and paclitaxel is one established treatment approach in ovarian cancer.

OCCC may respond differently to chemotherapy compared with some other ovarian cancer subtypes. Advanced disease can be particularly challenging, and researchers continue to investigate ways to improve treatment outcomes.

Targeted therapy and other approaches

Depending on the clinical situation and relevant test results, targeted treatments may be considered. Their suitability and evidence differ according to the treatment setting.

Immunotherapy and other newer approaches are also being investigated in clinical research. These should not be confused with established standard treatments for every person with OCCC.

There is no single treatment plan for everyone.

Treatment decisions should be made with a gynaecological oncology team. Patients can ask about the purpose of each treatment, expected benefits, possible side effects, alternatives and whether a clinical trial is appropriate.

06 / RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENTS

What is research exploring?

Because OCCC is less common than some other ovarian cancer subtypes, it has been represented less extensively in many large clinical trials. This creates challenges when researchers try to establish which treatments work best specifically for this diagnosis.

Current research is exploring several areas:

Molecular changes and tumour biology

Researchers are studying alterations involving ARID1A, PI3K/AKT/mTOR and other molecular pathways. The aim is to understand how these changes influence tumour development and whether they can help identify potential treatment targets.

Immunotherapy

Researchers are investigating whether immunotherapy, alone or in combination with other treatments, could benefit selected patients with OCCC.

New treatment combinations

Studies are examining combinations of existing and investigational treatments in an effort to improve responses, particularly in advanced or recurrent disease.

Research is not the same as established treatment.

A promising laboratory result or early clinical-trial finding does not automatically mean a treatment is effective or approved. Research findings must be evaluated through appropriately designed studies.

07 / CLINICAL TRIALS

Exploring clinical trials

Clinical trials are research studies that investigate new treatments, combinations, diagnostic approaches or ways of improving cancer care.

For a less common cancer such as OCCC, clinical trials can help researchers address unanswered questions and evaluate treatments that may not yet be part of routine care.

What should you check before considering a trial?

  • Which cancer type and stage the trial includes
  • The phase and purpose of the study
  • Whether recruitment is currently open
  • Where the trial is being conducted
  • Eligibility requirements
  • Potential benefits, risks and alternatives

Participation is voluntary. A clinical trial is not automatically better than standard treatment, and eligibility does not guarantee a particular outcome.

08 / START A CONVERSATION

Questions you may want to ask your doctor

Understanding a diagnosis can take time. These questions may help you prepare for conversations with your healthcare team.

  • Has the diagnosis been confirmed through pathology?
  • What stage is the cancer, and what does that mean?
  • Are any additional pathology or molecular tests needed?
  • What are the goals of the proposed treatment?
  • How will treatment response be assessed?
  • Are there relevant clinical trials for my diagnosis?
  • What support is available for managing side effects?
