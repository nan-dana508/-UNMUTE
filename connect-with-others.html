You Don't Have to Face This Alone

A cancer diagnosis can sometimes make the world feel smaller. Connecting with people who understand what you are going through can help you feel heard, supported and less alone. Explore support groups, peer communities and safe spaces where patients, survivors and caregivers can connect.

Finding People Who Understand

Everyone's cancer journey is different, but you do not have to navigate it without support. Some people find comfort in speaking with someone who has experienced a similar diagnosis. Others prefer a structured support group, an online community or a space where family members can learn and connect.

There is no single right way to seek connection. Explore the options below and choose what feels comfortable and appropriate for you.

Cancer Support Groups

Support groups bring together people affected by cancer to share experiences, discuss challenges and learn from one another in a supportive environment.

Hospital-Based Support Groups

Some cancer hospitals and treatment centres facilitate patient-support activities, counselling sessions or group meetings.

  • Ask your hospital's social worker or counsellor about available groups.
  • Check whether meetings are conducted in person or online.
  • Ask whether family members or caregivers can participate.

Community and Non-Profit Organisations

Cancer-focused organisations may offer emotional support, patient guidance, community programmes and connections to other support services.

  • Look for organisations with a clear identity and contact information.
  • Ask about the type of support they provide.
  • Confirm whether their programmes are currently active.

Peer-to-Peer Support

Sometimes, speaking to just one person who has been through something similar can make a meaningful difference.

Connect With Someone Who Understands

Peer support can help patients speak openly about the emotional and practical challenges of living with cancer. A peer may share their personal experience and offer understanding without judgement.

  • Ask your treatment centre whether peer-support connections are available.
  • Look for programmes that have clear consent and privacy practices.
  • Choose whether you prefer someone with a similar diagnosis, age group or treatment experience.

Support at Different Stages

Your needs may change throughout diagnosis, treatment, recovery and survivorship. You may find it helpful to connect with people at a similar stage of their journey.

  • People newly diagnosed may seek guidance and reassurance.
  • People undergoing treatment may value shared coping experiences.
  • Survivors may seek connection during follow-up and life after treatment.

Online Communities

Online communities can make support more accessible, especially when travel, distance or treatment schedules make in-person meetings difficult.

Moderated Online Support Spaces

Some organisations and patient communities offer online groups, virtual meetings or discussion spaces for people affected by cancer.

  • Prefer communities with active moderators and clear community guidelines.
  • Check how membership and personal information are handled.
  • Understand whether the group is public, private or invitation-based.

Finding the Right Online Community

Not every online group will be the right fit. Look for a community that respects different experiences and encourages informed, compassionate conversations.

  • Check who manages the group and how concerns are reported.
  • Be cautious of claims promising guaranteed cures or treatment outcomes.
  • Remember that personal experiences are not medical advice.

Support for Young Adults

Facing cancer at a younger age can bring challenges that may feel different from those experienced by older adults. Finding people in a similar stage of life can help.

Connecting With Other Young Patients

Young adults may want to connect with others who understand the experience of managing cancer alongside education, work, relationships and changing plans for the future.

  • Ask your hospital about age-specific support programmes.
  • Look for moderated communities for young adults affected by cancer.
  • Choose spaces where you feel comfortable sharing at your own pace.

Life Beyond Treatment

The end of active treatment does not always mean that every challenge disappears. Some young survivors may want support with returning to studies or work, relationships, confidence and adjusting to life after cancer.

  • Explore survivorship and young-adult support communities.
  • Seek emotional support when adjusting to life after treatment.
  • Remember that everyone's recovery and adjustment are different.

Staying Safe While Connecting

Support communities can be valuable, but it is important to protect your privacy and make informed choices when interacting with people online or in person.

  • Protect personal information: Avoid sharing medical records, identification numbers, addresses, phone numbers or other sensitive details in public groups.
  • Be cautious of medical claims: Personal experiences are not clinical evidence. Discuss treatment decisions and medical advice with your healthcare team.
  • Watch out for scams: Be careful of people promising guaranteed cures, selling unverified treatments or requesting money under pressure.
  • Check community guidelines: Look for clear moderation policies, reporting options and respectful behaviour.
  • Set your own boundaries: You do not have to share your story, answer personal questions or remain in a group that makes you uncomfortable.

Sometimes, Being Understood Is a Kind of Support

You may find support in a group, a conversation with one person or a community that simply makes you feel less alone. Take your time, explore what feels right and remember that you deserve connection, understanding and respect throughout your journey.
