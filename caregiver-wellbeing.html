You Deserve Care, Too

Supporting someone through cancer can be one of the most meaningful things you do, but it can also be emotionally and physically demanding. Between appointments, responsibilities and uncertainty, your own needs can easily get pushed aside.

Your feelings matter. Your health matters. You deserve time to rest, people to lean on and support of your own. This space is for you, too.

Taking Care of the Person Behind the Care

Caregiving can bring a mixture of love, responsibility, fear, hope and exhaustion. Looking after yourself does not mean caring any less about someone else. Explore these six topics to understand your needs and find healthier ways to navigate your caregiving journey.

Topic 01

Understanding Caregiver Stress

Caring for someone with cancer can involve many responsibilities, from attending appointments and managing everyday tasks to providing emotional support. Over time, these responsibilities can become overwhelming.

Caregiver stress is the physical and emotional strain that can develop when the demands of caring for someone begin to exceed the time, energy and support available to you.

Why can caregiving become stressful?

  • Uncertainty: Worrying about treatment, test results, changes in health and what the future may bring.
  • Multiple responsibilities: Balancing caregiving with employment, education, parenting, household tasks and other commitments.
  • Emotional pressure: Trying to remain strong for someone else while managing your own feelings.
  • Physical demands: Frequent hospital visits, disrupted sleep and long periods of providing assistance.
  • Financial concerns: Managing treatment-related expenses, travel costs or changes in household income.
  • Feeling alone: Believing that others do not understand what you are experiencing or that you must handle everything yourself.

How might caregiver stress affect you?

  • Feeling constantly worried, overwhelmed or unable to relax.
  • Having difficulty concentrating or making everyday decisions.
  • Experiencing changes in sleep, appetite or energy levels.
  • Becoming more irritable, withdrawn or emotionally exhausted.
  • Finding less time for friendships, hobbies and personal interests.

What can help?

  • Acknowledge that caregiving can be difficult instead of expecting yourself to manage everything perfectly.
  • Identify the responsibilities that are causing the most stress.
  • Talk to someone you trust about how you are feeling.
  • Ask family members or friends to take on specific tasks.
  • Make time for regular breaks, even when your schedule is demanding.
  • Seek professional support if stress begins affecting your daily life.

Remember: Feeling stressed does not mean you are failing as a caregiver. It means you are human and may need more support.

Topic 02

Recognising Caregiver Burnout

When caregiving responsibilities continue without enough rest, support or recovery time, prolonged stress can develop into caregiver burnout.

Burnout can affect your physical health, emotional wellbeing and ability to manage everyday activities. Recognising the signs early can help you seek support before the strain becomes more difficult to manage.

Common signs of caregiver burnout

  • Persistent exhaustion: Feeling physically or emotionally drained even after resting.
  • Emotional changes: Increased irritability, frustration, sadness, anxiety or feeling disconnected.
  • Sleep difficulties: Struggling to fall asleep, waking frequently or feeling tired throughout the day.
  • Withdrawal: Avoiding friends, family or activities you previously enjoyed.
  • Neglecting personal needs: Skipping meals, postponing medical appointments or consistently ignoring your own health.
  • Feeling overwhelmed: Finding even small responsibilities difficult to manage.
  • Loss of motivation: Feeling unable to continue with responsibilities that previously felt manageable.

What should you do if you recognise these signs?

  • Take your feelings seriously instead of dismissing them as something you simply have to tolerate.
  • Speak with someone you trust about the pressure you are experiencing.
  • Identify tasks that can be shared or temporarily reduced.
  • Arrange regular breaks and protect time for rest.
  • Consider speaking with a doctor or mental health professional if symptoms persist or interfere with daily life.

When to seek immediate help

If you feel unable to keep yourself safe, experience thoughts of harming yourself or feel in immediate danger, seek urgent professional help or contact local emergency services.

Remember: You do not need to reach a breaking point before asking for help. Your wellbeing deserves attention at every stage.

Topic 03

Looking After Your Wellbeing

Self-care is not about being selfish or stepping away from someone who needs you. It is about recognising that you have physical, emotional and personal needs that deserve attention, too.

You do not need an elaborate routine or hours of free time to care for yourself. Small, realistic habits can help you maintain your wellbeing while navigating caregiving responsibilities.

Look after your physical health

  • Try to maintain regular meals and stay hydrated.
  • Prioritise sleep and rest whenever possible.
  • Make time for gentle physical activity that suits your health and circumstances.
  • Attend your own medical appointments and do not ignore persistent symptoms.
  • Avoid relying on excessive caffeine or other unhealthy habits to push through exhaustion.

Make space for your emotional wellbeing

  • Give yourself permission to acknowledge difficult feelings.
  • Talk to someone you trust instead of keeping every concern to yourself.
  • Consider journalling or other activities that help you process your thoughts.
  • Take short breaks from caregiving responsibilities when possible.
  • Consider counselling or professional mental health support if you need it.

Stay connected to your own life

  • Make time for friendships and relationships outside caregiving.
  • Continue hobbies, interests and activities that bring you joy.
  • Maintain personal goals related to education, work or other areas of your life when possible.
  • Allow yourself to enjoy positive moments without feeling guilty.
  • Remember that you are an individual with an identity beyond your caregiving role.

Create realistic self-care goals

Rather than expecting yourself to change everything at once, start with small actions. A proper meal, a short walk, a conversation with a friend or a few minutes of quiet can be a meaningful beginning.

Remember: You are allowed to have moments of happiness, rest and normalcy even when someone you love is going through a difficult time.

Topic 04

Sharing Responsibilities

Caregiving often becomes overwhelming when one person feels responsible for everything. Sharing responsibilities can reduce pressure, create a more supportive environment and help ensure that the patient's needs are met consistently.

Start by identifying what needs to be done

Write down the responsibilities involved in supporting the person. These may include:

  • Hospital appointments and transportation.
  • Medication and appointment reminders, when agreed upon with the patient.
  • Meal preparation and grocery shopping.
  • Household tasks and errands.
  • Childcare or support for other family members.
  • Administrative tasks, bills and paperwork.
  • Companionship and emotional support.

How to share the workload

  • Have an open conversation: Discuss what needs to be done and who is available to help.
  • Assign specific tasks: Instead of asking someone to help generally, identify a clear responsibility they can take on.
  • Consider everyone's capacity: Take work schedules, distance, finances and other responsibilities into account.
  • Create a shared schedule: Use a calendar or simple task list to coordinate appointments and responsibilities.
  • Review arrangements: Needs may change during treatment, so check whether the existing plan is still manageable.
  • Respect the patient's preferences: Involve them in decisions about who helps and how their personal information is shared.

How to ask others for help

Asking for assistance can feel uncomfortable, especially if you are used to managing everything independently. However, people around you may be willing to help but unsure what is needed.

You could try saying:

  • "Could you accompany them to their appointment next week?"
  • "Would you be able to prepare dinner on Wednesdays?"
  • "Could you spend a little time with them while I take a break?"
  • "Can you help organise the upcoming appointments?"

Remember: You do not have to be the only person holding everything together. Accepting help is a healthy part of caregiving.

Topic 05

Managing Difficult Emotions

Supporting someone with cancer can bring emotions that are difficult to understand or express. You may feel worried about the future, frustrated by circumstances, guilty about taking time for yourself or helpless when you cannot change what someone is going through.

These feelings do not make you a bad caregiver. They are human responses to a challenging situation.

Understanding some common emotions

  • Fear and uncertainty: Worrying about treatment outcomes, changes in health and the future.
  • Guilt: Feeling guilty when you rest, enjoy yourself or cannot meet every expectation.
  • Frustration: Feeling overwhelmed by responsibilities, delays, changing plans or circumstances outside your control.
  • Helplessness: Wanting to make everything better but not knowing how.
  • Sadness: Feeling distressed about changes in your loved one's life and the uncertainty surrounding the journey.
  • Anticipatory grief: Experiencing grief or fear about possible future losses before they happen.

Ways to navigate difficult feelings

  • Allow yourself to acknowledge your emotions without judging them.
  • Talk to someone you trust rather than keeping everything inside.
  • Write down your thoughts if expressing them aloud feels difficult.
  • Focus on what is within your control instead of trying to predict every possible outcome.
  • Take breaks when emotions become overwhelming.
  • Seek professional counselling or mental health support when needed.

Let go of the expectation to be strong all the time

You may feel pressure to remain positive, composed and dependable for everyone around you. But you are allowed to have difficult days. You can love someone deeply and still feel exhausted, frightened or frustrated by the situation.

You do not have to hide every emotion to be a supportive caregiver.

Remember: Your feelings deserve space, too. You can care deeply for someone while also acknowledging that this journey is difficult for you.

Topic 06

Finding Support for Yourself

Caregivers need support just as much as the people they care for. You do not have to navigate every responsibility, concern or emotion on your own. Reaching out can help you feel understood and less isolated.

Where can you find support?

  • Family and friends: Share your concerns with people you trust and let them know when you need help.
  • Caregiver support groups: Connect with others who understand the challenges of supporting someone with cancer.
  • Professional counselling: A qualified mental health professional can help you work through stress, anxiety, grief and other difficult emotions.
  • Hospital support services: Ask the patient's healthcare team whether social workers, counsellors or caregiver support services are available.
  • Community organisations: Explore trusted cancer organisations that provide information, practical assistance or peer connections.
  • Online communities: Consider moderated communities that offer opportunities to connect with other caregivers while protecting your privacy.

What can support groups offer?

  • A space to share experiences with people facing similar challenges.
  • Practical ideas for managing everyday caregiving responsibilities.
  • Emotional understanding and a sense of connection.
  • An opportunity to learn how others access support and resources.

Choosing a safe support space

  • Look for groups with clear moderation and community guidelines.
  • Protect personal and medical information when participating online.
  • Be cautious of anyone promising guaranteed treatment outcomes or promoting unverified medical advice.
  • Use qualified healthcare professionals for medical decisions rather than relying solely on peer experiences.
  • Remember that you can leave a group or community if it makes you uncomfortable.

It is okay to ask for professional help

If stress, anxiety, sadness or exhaustion begins interfering with your daily life, consider speaking with a qualified professional. Seeking support is not a sign of weakness. It is one way of taking your own wellbeing seriously.

Remember: You deserve a support system of your own. You should not have to carry the emotional weight of caregiving alone.

You Matter Beyond Your Caregiving Role

Caring for someone through cancer can change your everyday life, but it should not mean losing sight of yourself. You deserve rest, understanding, meaningful relationships and opportunities to continue living your own life. Take things one day at a time, ask for help when you need it and remember that your wellbeing matters, too.
