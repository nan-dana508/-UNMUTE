Understanding Ovarian Cancer.

Ovarian cancer is not a single disease. It includes different types of cancers affecting the ovaries and related reproductive tissues. Understanding its symptoms, diagnosis, stages and treatment options can help patients and families make more informed decisions.

This guide brings together medically reviewed information, research resources and support pathways to help make a complex diagnosis easier to understand.

01. Overview of Ovarian Cancer

Ovarian cancer is a group of cancers that develop in the ovaries or closely related reproductive tissues. These include the fallopian tubes and the peritoneum, the tissue lining the abdominal cavity.

The ovaries are two small reproductive organs located on either side of the uterus. They produce eggs and hormones such as oestrogen and progesterone.

Where can these cancers begin?

Ovaries

Cancers can develop from the cells that form the surface or internal tissues of the ovaries.

Fallopian Tubes

Many high-grade serous carcinomas, the most common type of epithelial ovarian cancer, are now believed to originate in the cells of the fallopian tube, particularly its fimbrial end.

Peritoneum

Primary peritoneal cancer develops in the lining of the abdominal cavity. It is closely related to epithelial ovarian and fallopian tube cancers.

How does ovarian cancer develop?

Cancer develops when abnormal cells acquire changes that allow them to grow and divide without normal control. These cells may form a tumour and, in some cases, spread to surrounding tissues or other parts of the body.

Ovarian cancer is not one uniform disease. Different tumour types have different biological characteristics, patterns of growth and responses to treatment.

Why understanding the type matters: Knowing the exact origin and subtype of a tumour is essential because treatment approaches can differ significantly between ovarian cancer types.

02. Types & Subtypes of Ovarian Cancer

Ovarian tumours are classified according to the cells from which they develop. The three broad groups are epithelial tumours, germ cell tumours and sex cord-stromal tumours.

1. Epithelial ovarian cancers

Epithelial ovarian cancers develop from cells covering the ovary or from related reproductive tissues. They represent the majority of ovarian cancers.

Important subtypes include:

High-Grade Serous Carcinoma (HGSC)

A common and biologically aggressive epithelial subtype. Many cases are associated with changes in TP53 and may involve BRCA1, BRCA2 or other DNA-repair pathway abnormalities.

Low-Grade Serous Carcinoma (LGSC)

A distinct, generally slower-growing subtype. It may be associated with serous borderline tumours and changes in the MAPK pathway, including KRAS, BRAF and NRAS.

Endometrioid Carcinoma

A subtype that can sometimes be associated with endometriosis. Its molecular characteristics and treatment considerations vary.

Clear Cell Carcinoma

A distinct subtype also associated in some cases with endometriosis. Its behaviour and response to treatment can differ from other epithelial cancers.

Mucinous Carcinoma

A less common subtype. Specialists must determine whether a mucinous tumour began in the ovary or spread there from another organ, such as the gastrointestinal tract.

Low-grade versus high-grade serous carcinoma

Feature LGSC HGSC
Growth pattern Generally slower growing Generally faster growing
Biology Often associated with MAPK pathway changes Frequently associated with TP53 abnormalities
Chemotherapy response Often less responsive to conventional platinum-based chemotherapy Often initially responsive to platinum-based chemotherapy
Other treatment approaches Hormone therapy and selected targeted treatments may be considered PARP inhibitors and bevacizumab may be considered in selected patients

2. Germ cell tumours

Germ cell tumours develop from cells involved in egg production. They are relatively uncommon and often occur in younger patients.

Examples include:

  • Dysgerminoma.
  • Yolk sac tumour.
  • Immature teratoma.
  • Mixed germ cell tumours.
  • Embryonal carcinoma, which is rare.

3. Sex cord-stromal tumours

These develop from supporting tissues of the ovary, including cells involved in hormone production.

Examples include:

  • Adult and juvenile granulosa cell tumours.
  • Sertoli-Leydig cell tumours.
  • Steroid cell tumours.

What are borderline ovarian tumours?

Borderline ovarian tumours are also called tumours of low malignant potential. They show abnormal cell growth but do not demonstrate the destructive stromal invasion characteristic of invasive ovarian carcinoma.

Serous borderline tumours can sometimes be associated with the development of low-grade serous carcinoma, but most do not progress to invasive cancer.

Important: Imaging and blood markers cannot establish the exact ovarian cancer subtype. Histopathological examination of tissue is essential for accurate classification.

03. Signs & Symptoms

Ovarian cancer symptoms can be subtle and may overlap with common digestive, urinary or gynaecological conditions. Some people experience symptoms early, while others may not notice changes until the disease has progressed.

Common symptoms

Persistent bloating

Frequent or persistent abdominal bloating, swelling or an increase in abdominal size.

Pelvic or abdominal pain

Pain, discomfort or pressure in the lower abdomen or pelvic region.

Feeling full quickly

Difficulty eating normally, reduced appetite or feeling full after eating small amounts.

Urinary changes

Increased urinary frequency, urgency or difficulty postponing urination.

Other possible symptoms

  • Indigestion or persistent stomach discomfort.
  • Constipation or changes in bowel habits.
  • Nausea.
  • Lower back pain.
  • Unexplained fatigue.
  • Unexplained weight loss.
  • Changes in menstrual patterns.
  • Unusual vaginal bleeding, including bleeding after menopause.
  • Pain during sexual intercourse.

Symptoms in more advanced disease

Depending on the extent of disease, some patients may develop fluid accumulation in the abdomen, known as ascites. Fluid may also accumulate around the lungs, potentially causing breathlessness.

When should someone seek medical advice?

New, persistent, frequent or worsening symptoms should be discussed with a healthcare professional, particularly when they are unusual for the individual.

Keeping a symptom diary can help people describe when symptoms started, how often they occur and whether they are getting worse.

Remember: These symptoms do not automatically mean ovarian cancer. Many common conditions can cause similar symptoms. However, persistent or concerning changes deserve medical evaluation. Severe sudden abdominal pain, persistent vomiting or significant breathing difficulty require urgent medical attention.

04. Risk Factors

A risk factor is something associated with a higher likelihood of developing a disease. Having a risk factor does not mean someone will definitely develop ovarian cancer, and some people develop the disease without any recognised risk factors.

1. Age

The risk of epithelial ovarian cancer generally increases with age, and many cases occur after menopause. However, ovarian cancers can also occur in younger people, particularly certain germ cell tumours.

2. Family history

A family history of ovarian, fallopian tube or primary peritoneal cancer may increase risk. Certain patterns of breast, colorectal and endometrial cancers in a family may also suggest an inherited cancer predisposition.

Family history on either the mother's or father's side can be relevant.

3. Inherited genetic changes

Some inherited gene changes increase the risk of ovarian cancer. These include:

  • BRCA1.
  • BRCA2.
  • BRIP1.
  • RAD51C.
  • RAD51D.
  • Genes associated with Lynch syndrome, including MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 and PMS2.

Genetic counselling and testing may be appropriate depending on personal and family history.

4. Endometriosis

Endometriosis is associated with an increased risk of certain ovarian cancer subtypes, particularly clear cell and endometrioid carcinomas. Most people with endometriosis do not develop ovarian cancer.

5. Reproductive and hormonal factors

Factors associated with the number of lifetime ovulatory cycles may influence risk. Associations include never having had a full-term pregnancy, early menstruation and later menopause.

6. Menopausal hormone therapy

Some forms of menopausal hormone therapy, particularly use over longer periods, have been associated with ovarian cancer risk. The association varies according to the type and duration of therapy.

Other associated factors

  • Overweight or obesity, with associations varying by tumour type.
  • Some inherited syndromes, including Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.
  • Height, which has been associated with a small difference in risk.

Factors associated with lower risk

Some factors have been associated with a lower population-level risk of ovarian cancer, including:

  • Pregnancy carried to term.
  • Breastfeeding.
  • Use of oral contraceptive pills.
  • Tubal ligation.
  • Removal of fallopian tubes in selected circumstances.

Important: These associations should not be treated as personal prevention recommendations. No approach guarantees that ovarian cancer will be prevented. People with inherited risk should discuss individualised risk assessment and preventive options with appropriate specialists.

05. Screening

Screening means testing people who do not have symptoms to look for signs of a disease before it becomes apparent.

Unlike some other cancers, there is currently no routine ovarian cancer screening test proven to reduce ovarian cancer deaths among average-risk people without symptoms.

CA-125 blood test

CA-125 is a protein that may be elevated in the blood of some people with ovarian cancer.

However:

  • Not all ovarian cancers produce elevated CA-125.
  • Early-stage disease may have normal CA-125 levels.
  • Non-cancerous conditions can also raise CA-125.
  • An elevated result alone cannot diagnose ovarian cancer.

CA-125 may be used during the investigation of a suspected ovarian mass and for monitoring selected patients whose levels were elevated previously.

Transvaginal ultrasound

Transvaginal ultrasound uses sound waves to examine the ovaries and surrounding pelvic structures. It can help assess the size and appearance of an ovarian mass.

However, ultrasound cannot reliably distinguish every benign mass from a malignant tumour.

Why are CA-125 and ultrasound not routine screening tests?

Large studies evaluating CA-125 and transvaginal ultrasound have not demonstrated a reduction in ovarian cancer mortality from routine screening in average-risk asymptomatic populations.

Screening can also lead to false-positive results, unnecessary investigations and potentially unnecessary surgery.

What about people with inherited high risk?

People with BRCA1, BRCA2, Lynch syndrome or other relevant inherited risk factors may need specialist genetic counselling and individualised risk management.

CA-125 and ultrasound have not been established as effective mortality-reducing screening methods even in high-risk populations. A specialist may discuss individual circumstances and risk-reducing options.

Does a Pap test screen for ovarian cancer?

No. A Pap test is designed to screen for cervical cell changes and cervical cancer. It does not screen for ovarian cancer.

Remember: Screening and diagnostic evaluation are different. Someone experiencing persistent symptoms may need medical investigation even though routine screening is not recommended.

06. Diagnosis

Diagnosing ovarian cancer involves combining clinical assessment, imaging, laboratory investigations and examination of tissue. No single blood test or scan can establish every aspect of the diagnosis.

1. Medical history and physical examination

A healthcare professional may ask about symptoms, their duration, menstrual and reproductive history, previous medical conditions and family history.

Abdominal and pelvic examinations may help identify masses, tenderness or other abnormalities. However, examination alone cannot confirm or exclude ovarian cancer.

2. Imaging investigations

Pelvic Ultrasound

Transvaginal or transabdominal ultrasound can assess ovarian masses, their structure and other pelvic abnormalities.

CT Scan

CT imaging can help assess the abdomen, pelvis and chest for disease extent and possible spread.

MRI

MRI may provide additional information about pelvic masses and selected areas requiring further assessment.

PET-CT

PET-CT may be used in selected situations. It is not required for every patient.

3. Blood tests and tumour markers

Different tumour markers may be considered depending on the suspected cancer subtype.

  • CA-125: Commonly used in evaluating and monitoring selected epithelial ovarian cancers.
  • AFP: May be useful in selected germ cell tumours.
  • Beta-hCG: May be elevated in certain germ cell tumours.
  • LDH: May be useful in evaluating some germ cell tumours, including dysgerminoma.
  • Inhibin A and B: May be considered in selected granulosa cell tumours.
  • CEA and CA19-9: May help investigate selected mucinous tumours and possible gastrointestinal origins.

Other tests, including blood counts and kidney and liver function tests, may be performed to assess general health and treatment suitability.

4. Biopsy and tissue diagnosis

A biopsy involves obtaining tissue or cells for examination by a pathologist.

Possible approaches include:

  • Image-guided core needle biopsy.
  • Laparoscopic biopsy.
  • Surgical removal of tissue during an operation.

In advanced epithelial ovarian cancer where chemotherapy is planned before surgery, tissue confirmation is generally obtained first. An image-guided core biopsy may be used when appropriate.

Fine needle aspiration obtains cells or fluid but may not provide enough tissue architecture to establish every feature of a tumour.

5. Histopathology

Histopathology is the examination of tissue under a microscope. It helps establish whether cancer is present and identify its type and subtype.

Pathology may assess:

  • Tumour type and subtype.
  • Grade, where applicable.
  • Evidence of invasion.
  • Features that help distinguish primary ovarian cancer from another cancer spreading to the ovary.
  • Selected immunohistochemical markers.

6. Genetic and molecular testing

Genetic testing may examine inherited changes in genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 and genes associated with Lynch syndrome.

Tumour testing may also examine BRCA changes, homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) and other molecular characteristics that may influence treatment options.

Three different questions: Diagnosis establishes what the tumour is. Staging assesses how far it has spread. Molecular testing looks for biological features that may influence treatment. These investigations complement one another.

07. Stages of Ovarian Cancer

Staging describes how far cancer has spread from its original location. Ovarian, fallopian tube and primary peritoneal cancers are commonly staged using the FIGO staging system.

There are four main stages, from Stage I to Stage IV. Each stage has further subdivisions.

Stage I — Cancer confined to the ovaries or fallopian tubes

Stage I means the cancer is confined to one or both ovaries or fallopian tubes.

  • Stage IA: Cancer is limited to one ovary or one fallopian tube, with the capsule intact and no tumour on its surface.
  • Stage IB: Cancer involves both ovaries or both fallopian tubes, without the additional features defining Stage IC.
  • Stage IC: Cancer remains confined to the ovaries or fallopian tubes but there is surgical spill, capsule rupture before surgery, surface tumour or malignant cells in abdominal fluid or washings.

Stage IC is further divided into:

  • IC1: Surgical spill during the operation.
  • IC2: Capsule rupture before surgery or tumour on the ovarian or tubal surface.
  • IC3: Malignant cells identified in ascites or peritoneal washings.

Stage II — Spread within the pelvis

Stage II means the cancer has extended to other tissues within the pelvis, below the pelvic brim.

  • Stage IIA: Extension or implants involving the uterus, fallopian tubes or ovaries.
  • Stage IIB: Spread to other pelvic intraperitoneal tissues.

Stage III — Spread beyond the pelvis

Stage III means the cancer has spread to the peritoneum outside the pelvis and/or to retroperitoneal lymph nodes.

  • Stage IIIA1: Spread to retroperitoneal lymph nodes only, confirmed by cytology or histology.
  • Stage IIIA2: Microscopic spread to the peritoneum beyond the pelvis, with or without lymph node involvement.
  • Stage IIIB: Visible peritoneal deposits beyond the pelvis measuring up to 2 cm, with or without lymph node involvement.
  • Stage IIIC: Visible peritoneal deposits beyond the pelvis measuring more than 2 cm, with or without lymph node involvement.

Stage IV — Distant spread

Stage IV means cancer has spread to distant sites outside the abdominal peritoneal spread pattern used to define Stage III.

  • Stage IVA: Pleural effusion with malignant cells confirmed by cytology.
  • Stage IVB: Spread to organs such as the liver or spleen tissue, or to distant organs and lymph nodes outside the abdominal cavity.

Stage versus grade

Stage

Describes the extent of cancer and where it has spread.

Grade

Describes how abnormal cancer cells look under a microscope and, in some tumour types, how they are expected to behave.

Important: Cancer found on the peritoneum or omentum outside the pelvis is generally considered Stage III, not automatically Stage IV. Spread to the surface of the liver or spleen is also different from invasion into the organ tissue itself.

Staging is based on clinical assessment, imaging and, when appropriate, surgical and pathological findings. When chemotherapy is given before surgery, the original disease extent and the response to treatment are assessed as separate matters.

Remember: Stage is not the same as prognosis, and it does not determine whether treatment is worthwhile. People with the same stage can have different tumour subtypes, treatment options and outcomes.

08. Treatment of Ovarian Cancer

Ovarian cancer treatment depends on the exact tumour type, stage, molecular characteristics, previous treatment and the person's overall health.

A treatment plan is usually developed by a multidisciplinary team. Surgery, chemotherapy, targeted treatment, hormone therapy and clinical trials may all have a role in selected situations.

A. Surgery

Surgery is an important treatment for many ovarian cancers. The operation depends on the location and extent of the tumour.

Procedures may include:

  • Removal of one or both ovaries and fallopian tubes.
  • Removal of the uterus when indicated.
  • Removal of the omentum.
  • Peritoneal biopsies and abdominal washings.
  • Removal of visible tumour deposits from affected tissues.
  • Assessment or removal of suspicious lymph nodes.

Cytoreductive or debulking surgery

When cancer has spread within the abdomen, cytoreductive surgery aims to remove as much visible disease as safely possible.

The preferred outcome, when achievable safely, is complete gross resection, meaning no visible cancer remains after surgery.

Primary surgery versus chemotherapy first

Some patients undergo surgery before chemotherapy. This is called primary cytoreductive surgery.

Others receive chemotherapy first to reduce the tumour burden. This is called neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Surgery may then be performed after reassessment and is called interval cytoreductive surgery.

Why are these approaches different? The decision depends on whether complete tumour removal is likely, the risks of a major operation, the extent of disease and the patient's fitness for surgery.

Fertility-sparing surgery

Selected patients with certain early-stage ovarian cancers or germ cell tumours may be candidates for fertility-sparing surgery. This can involve removing the affected ovary and fallopian tube while preserving the uterus and the other ovary.

It is not appropriate for every tumour or stage. Fertility goals should be discussed with the specialist team before treatment when possible.

B. Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy uses medicines to destroy cancer cells or prevent them from multiplying.

Platinum-based chemotherapy

A commonly used first-line combination for many epithelial ovarian cancers is:

  • Carboplatin: A platinum-based medicine that damages cancer-cell DNA.
  • Paclitaxel: A taxane medicine that interferes with cell division.

These medicines are commonly administered in cycles. Many first-line plans involve approximately six cycles, although schedules and treatment duration vary.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is given before surgery. It may be recommended when complete removal of visible disease is unlikely initially or when major surgery carries excessive risk.

Adjuvant chemotherapy

Adjuvant chemotherapy is given after surgery to treat possible remaining microscopic cancer cells and reduce the risk of recurrence.

Chemotherapy for recurrent disease

If cancer returns, additional chemotherapy may be considered. Possible medicines include platinum-based combinations, paclitaxel, pegylated liposomal doxorubicin and gemcitabine.

The choice depends on previous treatment, treatment response, the time since earlier platinum therapy, side effects and overall health.

C. Targeted therapy

Targeted medicines act on specific biological pathways or molecular features involved in cancer growth. They are suitable only for selected patients.

PARP inhibitors

PARP inhibitors interfere with DNA repair processes. They may be particularly useful in selected cancers with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations or other DNA repair deficiencies.

Examples include:

  • Olaparib.
  • Niraparib.
  • Rucaparib.

BRCA and HRD testing may help specialists identify patients who could benefit from selected PARP inhibitor treatments.

Bevacizumab

Bevacizumab blocks VEGF, a protein involved in the development of new blood vessels. It may be combined with chemotherapy and continued as maintenance treatment in selected patients.

Targeted treatments for low-grade serous carcinoma

Low-grade serous carcinoma can involve changes in the MAPK signalling pathway. Targeted treatments may be considered in selected patients.

  • Trametinib: A MEK inhibitor studied in recurrent LGSC.
  • Avutometinib and defactinib: A targeted combination that received accelerated US FDA approval in 2025 for a specific group of patients with KRAS-mutated recurrent LGSC following previous systemic treatment.

Approval and availability of these medicines vary by country.

D. Hormone therapy

Some ovarian cancers, particularly selected low-grade serous tumours, express hormone receptors.

Hormone medicines may include:

  • Letrozole.
  • Anastrozole.
  • Tamoxifen.

These may be considered in selected cases, including some recurrent LGSC settings. Hormone therapy is not appropriate for every ovarian cancer subtype.

E. Treatment differences between tumour types

Cancer type Common treatment approaches
High-grade serous carcinoma Surgery and platinum-taxane chemotherapy; selected patients may receive maintenance PARP inhibitors or bevacizumab.
Low-grade serous carcinoma Surgery, selected chemotherapy approaches, hormone therapy and targeted treatment in appropriate circumstances.
Endometrioid carcinoma Surgery and, depending on stage and risk, chemotherapy; molecular findings may influence treatment.
Clear cell carcinoma Surgery and individualised systemic treatment; clinical trials may be considered.
Mucinous carcinoma Treatment depends on tumour origin and pathology; gastrointestinal metastases must be distinguished from primary ovarian cancer.
Malignant germ cell tumours Selected fertility-sparing surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy, commonly BEP.
Sex cord-stromal tumours Surgery and selected chemotherapy or hormone therapy, depending on subtype and disease extent.

F. Maintenance treatment

Maintenance treatment is given after an initial response or remission to help delay recurrence or progression.

Depending on the individual situation, it may include:

  • PARP inhibitors in eligible patients.
  • Bevacizumab in selected treatment plans.
  • Hormone therapy in selected LGSC cases.
  • Observation and regular follow-up.

Maintenance treatment is not necessarily lifelong. Its duration depends on the medicine, evidence, response, tolerability and applicable treatment guidance.

G. Treatment for recurrent disease

Recurrent ovarian cancer means the cancer has returned after an earlier response to treatment. Further treatment may involve chemotherapy, targeted medicines, hormone therapy, surgery in selected patients or clinical trials.

Recurrence does not automatically mean that no further treatment is possible.

H. HIPEC

Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) involves circulating heated chemotherapy within the abdominal cavity during surgery.

It may be considered in selected patients undergoing interval cytoreductive surgery for advanced disease. It is not routinely appropriate for everyone.

I. Supportive and palliative care

Supportive care helps manage treatment-related symptoms and maintain quality of life. It may include pain management, nutrition support, nausea management, fatigue care and psychological support.

Palliative care can be provided alongside active cancer treatment. It does not automatically mean that treatment has stopped.

J. Possible treatment side effects

Treatment Possible side effects
Surgery Pain, fatigue, infection risk, bleeding and recovery-related difficulties.
Chemotherapy Nausea, fatigue, hair loss, lowered blood counts, infection risk, neuropathy and bowel changes.
PARP inhibitors Anaemia, fatigue, nausea and low blood counts.
Bevacizumab High blood pressure, bleeding, blood clots and impaired wound healing.
Hormone therapy Hot flashes, joint discomfort and medicine-specific effects.
MEK inhibitors Rash, diarrhoea, fatigue, swelling and other medicine-specific effects.

Remember: Treatment is individualised. Not everyone receives every treatment listed here. Patients should discuss possible benefits, risks and alternatives with their oncology team before making treatment decisions.

09. Research & Clinical Trials

Research is continually exploring better ways to understand, detect and treat ovarian cancer. Clinical trials help evaluate whether new medicines, combinations, procedures and supportive-care approaches are safe and effective.

For patients and families, it is important to understand what stage a research development has reached and whether it is an established treatment or still experimental.

Why does ovarian cancer research matter?

  • To improve early detection and diagnostic accuracy.
  • To understand different tumour subtypes.
  • To develop more effective treatments.
  • To overcome treatment resistance.
  • To reduce treatment-related side effects.
  • To improve quality of life and survivorship.
  • To improve outcomes for rare and under-researched subtypes.

Important areas of ongoing research

Antibody-drug conjugates

ADCs combine antibodies that recognise particular cell targets with anti-cancer medicines. Researchers are investigating additional targets and combinations.

Immunotherapy

Studies are investigating immune checkpoint inhibitors, combination treatments and other approaches that may help the immune system recognise cancer cells.

DNA repair research

Research is exploring PARP inhibitors, resistance mechanisms and additional vulnerabilities in DNA repair pathways.

Low-grade serous carcinoma

Researchers are studying MAPK pathway alterations, targeted medicines and treatment combinations for LGSC.

Early detection

Research includes blood-based biomarkers, circulating tumour DNA and other diagnostic approaches. These are not established routine screening tests.

Precision medicine

Molecular and genetic information may help identify which treatments are more likely to benefit particular tumour subtypes.

What is a clinical trial?

A clinical trial is a research study involving people. It evaluates an intervention such as a medicine, treatment combination, procedure, diagnostic method or supportive-care approach.

Clinical trials help researchers understand safety, side effects, effectiveness and possible improvements over existing approaches.

Understanding clinical trial phases

Phase I

Usually involves a relatively small group and primarily evaluates safety, dosage and how the treatment behaves in the body.

Phase II

Studies whether the treatment shows evidence of effectiveness against a particular disease while continuing safety evaluation.

Phase III

Usually involves larger groups and compares a treatment with standard care or another appropriate control.

Phase IV

Studies an approved treatment after wider use to gather additional safety and effectiveness information.

Who can participate?

Each trial has eligibility criteria. These may include cancer subtype, stage, age, previous treatment, molecular findings and general health.

Meeting some eligibility criteria does not guarantee enrolment. The research team must assess each person individually.

How to evaluate a clinical trial

  • Study title and registration number.
  • Research question and purpose.
  • Trial phase and study design.
  • Recruitment status.
  • Participating hospitals and locations.
  • Eligibility criteria.
  • Investigational treatment and comparison group.
  • Study sponsor and investigators.
  • Published results, where available.

Informed consent and patient rights

Joining a clinical trial is a personal decision. Before agreeing, participants should receive information about the study purpose, possible benefits, risks, alternatives, costs, visits, privacy and their rights.

Participants can ask questions and should have time to consider their decision. They may withdraw from a trial, subject to discussing any necessary medical follow-up with the research team.

Questions to ask before joining a trial

  1. What is the main purpose of the study?
  2. What phase is the trial in?
  3. Why might I be eligible?
  4. Is the treatment experimental or already approved for another use?
  5. What are the known risks and possible benefits?
  6. Is there a standard treatment alternative?
  7. Will I be randomly assigned to a treatment group?
  8. What additional tests and visits are required?
  9. Who will pay for treatment, tests and travel?
  10. What happens if I decide to withdraw?

How UNMUTE presents research updates

Research updates on UNMUTE should use a consistent format so that readers can understand the evidence behind each development.

Research should create informed hope, not false promises. A promising laboratory result is not an established treatment. An early clinical trial does not guarantee success in later studies. Personal experiences cannot prove that a treatment is effective.

10. Support & Resources

A cancer diagnosis affects much more than physical health. Patients and families may experience emotional distress, financial pressure, changes in relationships and uncertainty about the future.

Support should be available throughout the cancer journey, not only during treatment.

A. Emotional and psychological support

A diagnosis can bring fear, anxiety, sadness, anger, uncertainty and changes in body image or confidence.

Support may include:

  • Oncology counselling.
  • Clinical psychology and psychotherapy.
  • Psychiatric support when needed.
  • Hospital-based psycho-oncology services.
  • Family counselling.
  • Peer support communities.

B. Peer support and patient communities

Connecting with someone who understands the experience of cancer may help patients feel less isolated.

Peer support may involve:

  • Talking with someone with a similar diagnosis.
  • Joining moderated online support communities.
  • Participating in hospital-based support groups.
  • Connecting with caregivers and families.
  • Attending survivorship programmes.

A note about personal stories: One person's treatment response, recovery or side effects cannot predict another person's outcome. Personal experiences should never replace medical advice.

C. Practical support during treatment

Patients may need help with transport, hospital appointments, accommodation, food, medication access, childcare, household responsibilities and returning to education or employment.

Hospital social workers, patient navigators and welfare departments may help identify appropriate services.

F. Palliative care

Palliative care focuses on relieving symptoms, improving comfort and supporting quality of life.

It may include:

  • Pain management.
  • Nausea and vomiting management.
  • Nutrition support.
  • Fatigue and sleep management.
  • Emotional support.
  • Communication and decision-making support.
  • Family and caregiver support.

Palliative care can be provided alongside active cancer treatment. It does not automatically mean that cancer treatment has stopped.

G. Support for caregivers and families

Caregivers may take on responsibilities such as attending appointments, managing medicines, arranging transport and providing emotional support.

Caregiver support may include:

  • Counselling and emotional support.
  • Education about the diagnosis and treatment.
  • Help understanding medical instructions.
  • Respite and practical assistance.
  • Support groups for caregivers.

H. Survivorship and life after treatment

Completing a major phase of treatment can bring relief, but it can also bring new challenges.

Survivorship support may address:

  • Follow-up appointments and monitoring.
  • Long-term treatment effects.
  • Menopause and hormonal changes.
  • Fertility and reproductive health.
  • Sexual health and relationships.
  • Physical activity and nutrition.
  • Emotional recovery and fear of recurrence.
  • Returning to education, work and everyday activities.

I. Building safe support communities on UNMUTE

Cancer-related communities involve sensitive health information and people who may be vulnerable. An online support space needs clear safeguards.

  • Clear community guidelines.
  • User consent before sharing personal stories.
  • Privacy controls and options to use a pseudonym.
  • Moderation and reporting mechanisms.
  • Protection against harassment and exploitation.
  • Rules against unsolicited medical advice.
  • No promotion of unverified cures or miracle treatments.
  • No sharing another person's medical information without permission.

No one should have to navigate the cancer journey feeling completely alone. Patients deserve reliable information, respectful care, emotional support, practical assistance and the freedom to make informed decisions. Families and caregivers deserve support too.
