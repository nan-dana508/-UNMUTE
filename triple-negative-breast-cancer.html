BEYOND THE COMMON

Triple-Negative Breast Cancer.

Breast cancer is not a single disease. Understanding its different subtypes can make an important difference in how a diagnosis is understood and treated.

Explore what makes triple-negative breast cancer distinct, how it is diagnosed, available treatment approaches and the research shaping future possibilities.

01 / UNDERSTANDING THE DIAGNOSIS

What is triple-negative breast cancer?

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a distinct subtype of breast cancer defined by the absence of three important biomarkers in the cancer cells: estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2).

These biomarkers help doctors understand how a breast cancer may behave and which treatments are likely to work.

TNBC accounts for approximately 10–15% of breast cancers, although estimates vary between populations and studies.

It is often characterised by faster growth and a greater likelihood of recurrence during the first few years after diagnosis compared with some other breast cancer subtypes. However, individual outcomes vary considerably.

What does triple-negative mean?

It does not mean that the cancer is negative for all possible biomarkers. It specifically refers to ER-negative, PR-negative and HER2-negative results.

02 / WHAT MAKES IT DIFFERENT?

Understanding the three biomarkers

Breast cancer cells are tested for specific proteins and receptors. The results help determine which treatment approaches may be suitable.

Estrogen receptor (ER)

Estrogen receptors are proteins that allow cells to respond to the hormone estrogen. Some breast cancers use estrogen signals to support their growth. TNBC cells do not show the level of estrogen receptor expression required to be classified as ER-positive.

Progesterone receptor (PR)

Progesterone receptors allow cells to respond to progesterone. TNBC cells are also negative for this receptor.

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)

HER2 is a protein involved in cell growth. Some breast cancers produce too much HER2, making them eligible for HER2-targeted treatments. TNBC is classified as HER2-negative based on the accepted testing criteria.

Why does receptor status matter?

Because TNBC lacks these three treatment targets, conventional hormone therapies and standard HER2-targeted treatments are generally not effective against it. Other treatment approaches, including chemotherapy and immunotherapy in selected settings, are available.

TNBC and basal-like breast cancer

TNBC and basal-like breast cancer are sometimes discussed together, but they are not exactly interchangeable terms. TNBC is defined by clinical biomarker testing, while basal-like is a molecular classification based on patterns of gene expression.

03 / RECOGNISING THE SIGNS

Possible signs and symptoms

The symptoms of TNBC are generally similar to those of other breast cancer types. Some people may not notice any symptoms, which is why appropriate breast screening and medical evaluation are important.

  • A new lump or thickening in the breast
  • A change in the size or shape of the breast
  • Changes in the appearance of the breast skin
  • Nipple inversion or a change in nipple appearance
  • Unusual nipple discharge, particularly if bloody
  • A lump or swelling in the armpit
  • Persistent breast or nipple discomfort
When should you seek medical advice?

A new or unusual breast change should be evaluated by a healthcare professional. Most breast changes are not cancer, but it is important not to ignore persistent or unexplained changes.

04 / RISK FACTORS AND GENETICS

Who may be at increased risk?

The exact cause of TNBC is not fully understood. Like other breast cancers, it develops through changes in cells that affect their normal growth and division.

Certain factors are associated with a higher likelihood of developing TNBC. These include:

  • Inherited changes in genes such as BRCA1
  • A personal or family history of breast cancer
  • Younger age at diagnosis compared with some other breast cancer subtypes
  • Some population-level differences in incidence

The role of BRCA1 and BRCA2

Inherited pathogenic variants in BRCA1 and, less commonly, BRCA2 can increase the risk of breast and ovarian cancers. TNBC is particularly associated with inherited BRCA1 variants.

Genetic counselling and testing may be recommended depending on a person's age at diagnosis, family history, cancer characteristics and applicable clinical guidelines.

Important to remember

Not everyone with TNBC has an inherited BRCA mutation, and not everyone with a BRCA mutation will develop TNBC. A negative genetic test does not mean that a person cannot develop breast cancer.

05 / DIAGNOSIS AND TESTING

How is TNBC diagnosed?

Diagnosis involves clinical examination, breast imaging, tissue sampling and laboratory testing to establish the cancer type and its biomarker status.

Clinical examination

A healthcare professional may examine the breasts and surrounding lymph nodes, ask about symptoms and review personal and family medical history.

Imaging investigations

Depending on age, symptoms and clinical findings, investigations may include:

  • Mammography
  • Breast ultrasound
  • Breast MRI in selected circumstances

Additional imaging may be recommended when necessary to assess the extent of disease.

Breast biopsy

A biopsy involves collecting a tissue sample from the suspicious area. A pathologist examines the sample to determine whether cancer is present and identify its characteristics.

Biomarker testing

Laboratory testing is used to assess estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor and HER2 status. A diagnosis of TNBC is established when the results meet the accepted criteria for all three negative biomarkers.

HER2 testing may involve immunohistochemistry (IHC) and, when indicated, additional testing such as in-situ hybridisation (ISH).

Staging

Staging describes the extent of cancer in the body. Breast cancer staging considers factors such as tumour size, lymph node involvement and whether the disease has spread to distant organs.

TNBC can be diagnosed at different stages. Its receptor status is one factor considered alongside the stage and other tumour characteristics when planning treatment.

06 / TREATMENT APPROACHES

Understanding treatment options

TNBC treatment depends on the stage of disease, tumour size, lymph node involvement, biomarker and genetic test results, overall health and individual treatment goals.

Treatment may involve a combination of chemotherapy, surgery, radiation therapy, immunotherapy and selected targeted treatments.

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is an important treatment approach for TNBC. It may be given before surgery to reduce tumour size or after surgery to lower the risk of recurrence.

Treatment regimens may include medicines from the taxane, anthracycline, cyclophosphamide or platinum groups, depending on the clinical situation.

Neoadjuvant treatment

Neoadjuvant treatment is treatment given before surgery. It is commonly considered for certain larger or higher-risk TNBC tumours.

It can help shrink the tumour and provide information about how the cancer responds to treatment.

After neoadjuvant treatment, examination of the surgical specimen helps determine whether any residual invasive cancer remains. This information can influence subsequent treatment decisions.

Surgery

Surgical options may include:

  • Lumpectomy: Removal of the cancer with a margin of surrounding healthy tissue while preserving the breast.
  • Mastectomy: Removal of the breast, which may be recommended depending on the extent of disease and individual circumstances.

Lymph node assessment may also be part of surgery. The appropriate approach depends on the individual diagnosis.

Radiation therapy

Radiation therapy uses targeted radiation to destroy cancer cells. It may be recommended after breast-conserving surgery and in selected cases after mastectomy, depending on tumour and lymph node findings.

Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy helps the immune system recognise and attack cancer cells. Pembrolizumab, an immune checkpoint inhibitor, is used with chemotherapy in certain high-risk early-stage TNBC treatment settings.

Its use depends on the disease setting and applicable treatment guidance. It is not appropriate for every person diagnosed with TNBC.

Targeted treatments

Some people may be eligible for targeted treatments based on specific genetic or tumour characteristics.

Examples include:

  • PARP inhibitors: Medicines such as olaparib may be considered in selected patients with inherited BRCA1 or BRCA2 pathogenic variants.
  • Antibody-drug conjugates: Treatments such as sacituzumab govitecan may be used in certain advanced or metastatic TNBC settings.

Eligibility, approval and availability vary by country, treatment setting and individual test results.

There is no single treatment plan for everyone.

Treatment decisions should be made with a breast cancer specialist and the wider oncology team. Patients can ask about the purpose of each treatment, expected benefits, possible side effects and available alternatives.

07 / RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENTS

What is research exploring?

TNBC is a biologically diverse disease. Researchers are studying why different tumours behave differently and how treatment can be better matched to individual tumour characteristics.

Immunotherapy combinations

Research is investigating how immunotherapy can be combined with chemotherapy and other treatments to improve outcomes in different TNBC settings.

Antibody-drug conjugates

Antibody-drug conjugates are designed to deliver cancer-killing medicines to cells using an antibody that recognises a particular target. Researchers are studying existing and newer antibody-drug conjugates for different stages and subgroups of breast cancer.

Genetic and molecular research

Studies are examining inherited and acquired genetic changes, DNA repair pathways and other molecular features that may help identify treatment opportunities.

Personalised treatment approaches

Researchers are investigating ways to identify which patients are most likely to benefit from particular treatments, reduce unnecessary treatment exposure and improve long-term outcomes.

Research is not the same as established treatment.

A promising laboratory finding or early clinical-trial result does not automatically mean that a treatment is effective or approved. Findings must be evaluated through appropriately designed clinical studies.

08 / CLINICAL TRIALS

Exploring clinical trials

Clinical trials investigate new treatments, treatment combinations, diagnostic methods and approaches to improving cancer care.

TNBC is an active area of clinical research. Studies are investigating new immunotherapy combinations, targeted medicines, antibody-drug conjugates and approaches for recurrent or advanced disease.

What should you check before considering a trial?

  • Which breast cancer stage or subtype is included
  • The phase and purpose of the study
  • Whether recruitment is currently open
  • Where the trial is being conducted
  • Eligibility and biomarker requirements
  • Potential benefits, risks and alternatives
  • Whether participation affects standard treatment options

Participation is voluntary. A clinical trial is not automatically better than standard treatment, and eligibility does not guarantee a particular outcome.

09 / START A CONVERSATION

Questions you may want to ask your doctor

A diagnosis can bring many questions. These may help you prepare for conversations with your healthcare team.

  • Have ER, PR and HER2 results confirmed TNBC?
  • What is the stage and grade of the cancer?
  • Should I consider genetic counselling or BRCA testing?
  • Would treatment before surgery be appropriate?
  • What is the goal of each recommended treatment?
  • Would immunotherapy or targeted treatment be relevant to my diagnosis?
  • How will treatment response be assessed?
  • Are there clinical trials suitable for my situation?
  • What support is available for managing treatment side effects?
